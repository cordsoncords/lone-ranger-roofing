Burnet County sits out on the edge of Hill Country, open enough that a storm rolling in off the lake does not lose much steam before it hits a rooftop. Homeowners out here tend to lump the aftermath together under one label, storm damage, without thinking much about which part of the storm actually did the damage. That distinction matters more than it seems, because wind, hail, and lightning do not damage a roof the same way, and a roof can come through one of those forces fine while quietly failing under another.

We have inspected roofs across Burnet County after all three, and the patterns are different enough that treating storm damage as a single category is how real problems get missed.

## Hail: Damage That Hides in Plain Sight

Hail bruises shingles rather than breaking them outright. The impact knocks loose the granules that protect the asphalt layer underneath from sun and weather, leaving a soft spot that looks close to normal from the ground but has already lost part of what keeps it durable. Over the following months, that bruised area is where cracking and leaks tend to start, well after the storm that caused it is forgotten.

Because hail damage is subtle at first, it is the type most likely to get missed without a close inspection. A roof that "looks fine" after a hailstorm is not the same as a roof that came through unaffected.

## Wind: Damage You Can Sometimes Hear Before You See It

Strong wind gets underneath shingle tabs and breaks the seal holding them flat against the roof deck. Sometimes that shows up as a loose shingle flapping in the next breeze. More often, the seal breaks without any shingle visibly lifting off, which means water now has a path underneath the shingle line even though the roof looks intact from the street.

Burnet County's wide-open terrain means wind gusts here often hit roofs with less obstruction than they would in a denser neighborhood, which is part of why wind damage shows up on roofs that never took a direct hailstorm at all.

## Lightning: Rare, but Not Something to Rule Out

Lightning damage is far less common than hail or wind, but it is worth understanding because it looks nothing like the other two. A direct or near strike can crack decking, blow apart a section of shingles, or damage vents and flashing in a very localized spot rather than spreading across the roof. It is the kind of damage that is obvious once you know to look for it and easy to miss if an inspection stops at the wind and hail patterns homeowners expect.

## Why the Distinction Actually Matters

Insurance claims, repair scope, and even warranty coverage can depend on correctly identifying which force caused which damage. A [roof inspection](/residential-roofing/roof-inspection) that stops at the obvious hail pattern can miss a wind-lifted seal three sections over, or a lightning-related crack near a vent stack that has nothing to do with the hailstorm everyone remembers. We check for all three on every storm inspection in Burnet County, because a roof rarely takes damage from a single one of them at a time.

## FAQ

**Can wind damage a roof without any shingles blowing off?**
Yes. A broken seal underneath a shingle can let water in without the shingle itself ever visibly lifting, which is why a visual check from the ground often misses it.

**Is lightning damage covered by homeowners insurance?**
In most standard policies, yes, though it is worth confirming with your specific carrier since coverage details vary.

**How soon after a storm should I get my Burnet County roof inspected?**
As soon as it is safe to do so. Documenting damage close to the storm date makes it easier to tie repairs directly to that event for insurance purposes.

**Can one storm cause hail, wind, and lightning damage all at once?**
It happens more than people expect in Central Texas, since a single severe storm cell can produce all three within the same event.
