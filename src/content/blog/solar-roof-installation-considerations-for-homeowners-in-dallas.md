Solar interest in Dallas has climbed fast, driven by summer electric bills that seem to set a new record every July. What gets less attention is the roof underneath the panels, the part of the system that has to hold everything up for the next 20 to 25 years while the solar equipment sits on top of it. A solar company sells electricity generation. It does not always think about what happens to the shingles, the flashing, or the deck once their crew drills into it.

We have gone out to enough Dallas homes after a solar install to see the pattern. The panels work fine. The roof underneath them is the part nobody checked first, and by the time a leak shows up, tracing it back through a rack of panels is a far bigger job than it would have been before installation day.

This is the piece worth getting right before signing a solar contract: the roof comes first, and the installation should be planned around its real condition, not around a sales calendar.

## Why the Roof Comes Before the Panels

A solar installer's job is to mount panels and connect wiring. Most solar companies subcontract the roofing work or skip a real assessment altogether, relying on a quick look from the ground or a satellite image to scope the job. That works fine on a roof with years of life left in it. It becomes a problem on a roof that is already five to eight years from needing replacement, because pulling panels off to redo a roof later costs far more than doing the roof first.

A [roof inspection](/residential-roofing/roof-inspection) before a solar quote tells a Dallas homeowner what they are actually working with — deck condition, remaining shingle life, and whether the existing structure can handle the added weight and the mounting hardware. That inspection should happen before a solar contract gets signed, not after panels are already scheduled for delivery.

## What a Solar-Ready Roof Actually Looks Like in Dallas

A roof ready for solar installation has at minimum ten to fifteen years of expected life left, a deck free of rot or soft spots, and flashing details that can properly seal around the mounting brackets. Dallas roofs take a specific kind of punishment — intense UV exposure for most of the year, sudden hailstorms that can arrive with little warning, and heat cycling that stresses shingles and sealants over time.

Composition shingle roofs are the most common in Dallas neighborhoods, and they can support solar mounting as long as the deck underneath is sound. [Metal roofing](/residential-roofing/metal-roofing) is also a strong pairing for solar, since the standing seam design allows for clamp-based mounting that does not require penetrating the roofing material at all. For homeowners planning a roof replacement anyway, timing that replacement to happen right before a solar installation avoids the redundant labor of removing and reinstalling panels down the line.

### Roof Age Matters More Than the Panel Brand

Homeowners spend weeks comparing panel efficiency ratings and inverter brands, then give almost no thought to how old the roof underneath actually is. A 22-year-old shingle roof with five years left in it is not a good candidate for a 25-year solar investment, no matter how good the panels are. The roof will need work well before the solar equipment does, and that work becomes more complicated and more expensive with panels already mounted.

## Timing the Installation Around Your Roof's Real Age

The honest recommendation for most Dallas homeowners: if a [roof replacement](/residential-roofing/roof-replacement) is within five years of being needed, do it before solar goes up, not after. A new roof installed ahead of solar panels means the mounting hardware goes onto fresh materials with a full warranty period ahead of it, and there is no reason to remove panels for a reroof partway through their service life.

For homes with a roof that already has a decade or more of life remaining, solar can typically proceed on the existing roof without complication, provided the mounting is done correctly and the flashing details are sealed properly around every penetration point.

## What Happens When Something Goes Wrong Underneath the Panels

The most common issue we see after solar installs is water intrusion around mounting brackets that were not flashed correctly. A rack of panels sits low to the roof surface, which makes leaks hard to spot early since the water often travels along the deck before it shows up as a stain on a ceiling. By the time it is visible inside the house, the damage has usually been building for months.

This is where the installer matters as much as the equipment. A mounting job done by a crew that understands roofing first and solar wiring second uses proper flashing at every penetration and accounts for how water moves across a Dallas roof during a heavy downpour. If [storm damage repair](/residential-roofing/storm-damage-repair) is ever needed on a roof with panels already installed, that work takes longer and costs more, since panels typically need to come off before the roofing work can happen underneath them.

## FAQ

**Does adding solar panels void my roof's existing warranty?**
It can, depending on how the mounting hardware penetrates the roofing material and who performs the installation. Reading the manufacturer's warranty terms before installation, and using a mounting method that matches those terms, protects the coverage already in place.

**How much weight can a Dallas roof structure typically handle for solar?**
Most residential roof structures are built to handle solar panel loads without reinforcement, but this depends on the home's age and construction. A structural check as part of a pre-installation inspection confirms this rather than assuming it.

**Should I replace my roof before or after installing solar?**
Before, if the roof has less than ten years of expected life remaining. Installing solar on a roof that will need replacement soon means paying twice, once for the panels and again for the labor to remove and reinstall them during a reroof.

**Can solar panels be installed on any roofing material?**
Most materials can support solar with the right mounting hardware, though asphalt shingles and standing seam metal are the most common and straightforward options in the Dallas area. Flat or low-slope roofs require a different mounting approach entirely.
